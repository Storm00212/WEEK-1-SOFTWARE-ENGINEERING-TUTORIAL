Section 4 · Part 1 of 13

GlucoPlate: sugar-safe meals on a real budget

Builds a day of meals from 95 Kenyan dishes (ugali, sukuma wiki, githeri and more), priced in KSh, that respect each person’s carbohydrate allowance, sugar-impact limit and budget.

Requirements

  • Plan three meals a day within a budget
  • Different plan for different health levels
  • Explain each dish choice
  • Track weight and trends
  • Work on web and phone

Constraints

  • Zero hosting budget (student project)
  • Health advice carries real risk
  • Users on mobile data and small screens
  • One developer, limited time

Glycemic index (GI): a 0–100 score for how fast a food raises blood sugar. Lower is steadier.

Section 4 · Part 3 of 13

How the API is organised

The API is the list of requests the apps may make. Every address starts with /api and is grouped by topic, one group per backend module.

Rules every route follows

  • Action words mean the same everywhere: GET reads, POST creates, PATCH changes part of something, DELETE removes
  • Everything except sign-up, sign-in and the health check needs a login token
  • The user is taken from the token, never from what the request claims

Same shape every time

  • Errors always look like: message, short code, request ID
  • Each module has three parts: routes (receive), service (decide), repository (talk to the database)

One catalogue, five levels of care

Rules live in one shared place, so the server, web and phone all apply the same limits.

Ugali has a GI of 73, above all five limits. When a limit is set, the planner leaves it out. Someone at “critical” gets a stricter plan and a red “talk to your clinician” banner.

The same dish list gives each of the five demo people a different plan.

Section 4 · Part 12 of 13

How a plan is made

  1. Filter. Remove dishes above the person’s GI limit, and anything on their allergy or dislike list.
  2. Shortlist. For morning, afternoon and evening, keep the best-fitting, lower-cost dishes.
  3. Score combinations. Reward carbs up to the day’s allowance, protein and calories. Reward using the budget. Penalise higher average GI. Small bonus when a dish suits its meal.
  4. Check and explain. Show cost against budget, carbs against allowance, GI against limit, with a message if any is over.

Budget example: KSh 1,400 a week becomes about KSh 200 a day.

Section 4 · Part 13 of 13

Trade-offs accepted and what comes next

Accepted on purpose

  • One backend instead of five services: simpler and faster, harder to split later
  • Free hosting: costs nothing, limited power
  • Rule-based planner instead of AI: predictable and explainable

Still to do

  • Limit repeated login attempts (not in the code yet). The AI routes have a per-user limit, but it lives in memory and resets on restart
  • Restrict allowed web addresses; default is “any” until set
  • Free server sleeps after 15 idle minutes, so the first request is slow
  • Keep your own database backup copy (free Neon restores only the last 6 hours)
  • Finish M-Pesa payments

Section 6 · Part 4 of 6

Learn by hand first, then use AI as a tutor

Copy and paste

  • Paste a one-line request and run whatever returns
  • Paste errors back until it stops complaining
  • You can’t explain why it works
  • Each fix may break something else

Conversation

  • Say what you’re building and your limits
  • Ask why, then challenge: “What could go wrong? What else could I choose?”
  • Ask for code to be explained line by line
  • Check answers against your own design
  1. Build a tiny app by hand. A sign-in and a list, no AI. This teaches the layers and how data moves.
  2. Break it on purpose and read the errors until you can predict them.
  3. Bring in AI as a tutor. Ask it to explain, quiz you, and criticise your plan.
  4. Let it draft, you review. Compare every part with your design and test it.
Section 6 · Part 5 of 6

Vague request, vague result

Without a design

“Build an app that does so and so. Make no mistakes.”

It doesn’t know who uses it, what limits apply, which tools you use, or what a “mistake” is. So it guesses and returns something generic.

With a design

“I’m building GlucoPlate, a meal planner for people with diabetes in Kenya, used on phones with slow data. It plans three meals a day within a budget in KSh. Free hosting, one developer, two weeks. React and Vite, Express, PostgreSQL with Prisma. Users may only see and edit their own data, passwords are scrambled, no keys in the frontend. Payments are out of scope. Ask me questions first, then propose the routes and tables for me to approve.”

The design work from Sections 2 to 4 is what makes the request good.

Section 6 · Part 6 of 6

Before you accept AI-written code

  1. Does it match my design, my tools and my limits?
  2. Who is allowed to use this? Is the user taken from the login, not from the request?
  3. Where are the secret keys?
  4. What happens with empty, huge or harmful input?
  5. Does it run, and do I have a test that proves it?
  6. Can I explain each part to someone else? If not, ask the AI to teach it, or write it myself.

Never paste patient, client or secret data into an AI tool.

Planning your own prototype

Professional: an MVP (minimum viable product) is the smallest version that tests whether the idea works.

The simplest version that answers “does anyone need this?”

Keep

  • One main task done well
  • One type of user
  • Real data for one scenario

Leave out for now

  • Payments, badges, extra reports
  • Features for users you don’t have yet

One-page plan

  • Users and their top three tasks
  • Layers diagram
  • Time, cost, risk limits
  • Tools you can finish with

Four things to remember

  1. Ask who uses it and what limits you before choosing tools.
  2. Judge each layer on scale, time, security, and what you give up.
  3. Build the smallest version that proves the idea.
  4. Learn the basics by hand, then use AI as a tutor and assistant. Give it your design and check every answer.
